# Long-Term Outcome

The primary long-term outcome is that the collection is available to the historians of the future.

The term "collection" has a multi-layered meaning. In most instances, it will be digital images or a representations of the actual, physical sources, such as photographs, scans, or other digital surrogates, and not necessarily the sources themselves.

Ultimately, the long-term outcome is to create a sustainable and well-documented digital archive that can serve as a reliable resource for historians, researchers, and the general public for generations to come.

The formats should be to current best practices and widely accepted archival standards such as text files, jpg, png, and PDF/A.

Additionally, metadata should be comprehensive and follow established standards like Dublin Core or METS to ensure proper documentation and discoverability of the digital archive.

license should be clearly stated, preferably using open-source licenses such as CC BY 4.0 or MIT, to ensure that the digital archive can be freely accessed, shared, and reused by future researchers and the public.

Where will the data reside?

The data should be stored in secure, redundant, and geographically distributed locations to ensure long-term preservation and accessibility. Cloud storage solutions, institutional repositories, and dedicated archival servers are all viable options, provided they adhere to best practices for data integrity and security.

GitHub, archive.org, permanent.org, Wikimedia Commons, Wikidata, and other reputable digital repositories should be used for storing and preserving the digital archive to ensure its long-term accessibility and security. Ancestry.com and MyHeritage And the genealogy sites might be other possibilities. 

In that the sources, the digital results are all openly accessible, certain items may go to multiple outcomes. A particular institution might have an interest in the personal sources (primary sources) of just one individual in the collection. 

## Long term Outcome of the original physical source objects

The general view is to recycle the source objects unless there is a good reason for preserving the object. 

Primary sources that relate to certain individuals, such as Normon Armour and Walter Lowrie, will be offered to the Library, Princeton University as items that may be added to their existing collections for these individuals.

Items of museum quality will be offered to institutions That are likely to acquire the objects For example, the objects relating to George Armour might be offered to the Chicago Art Institute, and the items relating to the Kudashevs might be offered to one of the Hermitage institutions. 

It is hoped that the archivist, looking through the sources, will say, "Ah, this item could be interesting to somebody." In such cases, the item will be preserved and offered to the appropriate institution or individual who may find it valuable.

The final outcome for the Armour collection is that it possesses no physical objects. 

## Funding

The goal is that assessing and processing and preserving the data will be contracted and paid for by me. Subsequent updating and management and preservation of the data will be carried out by the organization or individuals responsible for the digital archive.
