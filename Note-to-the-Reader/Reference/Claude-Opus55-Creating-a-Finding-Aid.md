# Creating a Finding Aid

With AI's ability to search, locate, identify, scan, OCR, and do everything else, how does the finding aid change?

A finding aid is the map of the collection. It tells a stranger what's there, who made it, how it's organized, and where each thing lives, without opening a box. Archivists follow a standard called DACS (Describing Archives: A Content Standard). A family archive can use a lighter version of the same structure.

## 1. Build it while you arrange, not after
- **Keep a working inventory from the first box.** Each time you house a folder, log its box and folder number, a title, the dates, and a one-line note.
- **Give every container a permanent ID** (`Box 3, Folder 12`) and pencil it on the folder. The finding aid and the shelf must agree.
- **Keep a processing log** of what you moved, discarded, re-housed or found damaged. It becomes the processing note later.

## 2. Choose the level of detail
- **Collection level:** a single description of the whole. This is the minimum every collection needs.
- **Series level:** groups such as Correspondence, Photographs, Legal and Financial, and Recordings.
- **Folder level:** the usual target for a family archive.
- **Item level:** only for high-value or heavily used material, such as letters from a key ancestor or identified photographs. Describing every item stalls projects.

Describe the whole collection at a basic level first, then go deeper where it's worth it. Writing broad first and adding detail later is the "more product, less process" approach.

## 3. Write the sections, top-down

| Section | What it answers |
|---|---|
| **Title** | Whose and what, e.g. *Armour Family Papers, 1880–2025* |
| **Dates** | Inclusive span, plus bulk dates where most of the material falls (*bulk 1940–1975*) |
| **Extent** | How much: number of boxes, linear feet, item count, GB of files |
| **Creator(s)** | The people or households who made or gathered the material |
| **Location** | Where the boxes and files physically and digitally live |
| **Biographical note** | Short life sketches, places, relationships, and a simple family tree |
| **Scope and content** | What the collection covers, its strengths and gaps, notable items, and what's *not* here |
| **Arrangement** | The series list, and whether original order was kept |
| **Provenance** | Who held the material before, and how it came to you |
| **Access and use** | Restrictions on living people, sensitive records, and who holds copyright |
| **Related materials** | Other relatives' holdings, archives, genealogy databases, published works |
| **Processing note** | Who processed it, when, and what was weeded or re-housed |
| **Container list** | Box, folder, title, dates and notes, in shelf order |

Write the container list first, because it's the factual base. Then write scope and content, then the biographical note, and put the summary fields at the top last. You can't summarize well until you've seen everything.

## 4. Keep the description consistent
- **Dates:** `YYYY-MM-DD`, `YYYY-MM`, `YYYY`, `circa 1920`, `1930s`, or `undated`. Never leave a date blank without explanation.
- **Names:** a full form with the maiden name and life dates, e.g. *Armour, Mary (Smith), 1901–1988*. Keep a short authority list of names and use it everywhere.
- **Places:** the place name as it was then, with the modern name in brackets where it changed.
- **Uncertainty:** show guesses as guesses, e.g. *[probably Aunt Jean]*. Never present a guess as fact.
- **Titles:** describe what the thing is, e.g. *Letters from Tom to Mary during WWII*. Avoid vague titles like *Miscellaneous*. Where you can't avoid one, add a note explaining it.

## 5. Link the physical and digital
- **Scanned folders:** give the file name or folder path next to the box and folder number.
- **Matching names:** use the same ID in scan file names (`B03-F12-001.tif`) so anyone can trace a scan to its original and back.
- **Born-digital material:** record the source device, date range, file types and size.

## 6. Review with family
- **Circulate a draft to relatives** who can fix names, dates and relationships. They'll catch errors nobody else can.
- **Record oral information as you receive it,** noting who said it and when (*per cousin Ann, 2026-11*).

## 7. Publish and maintain
- **Format:** a single plain-text file, such as Markdown or HTML, that will still open in 50 years. Export a PDF too.
- **Paper copy:** keep a printed copy in Box 1 so the physical collection explains itself.
- **Share it:** with the successor you named and with any related repository.
- **Version it:** put a revision date at the top and a short change history. When something is added or moved, update the finding aid first.

## Quick test of a good finding aid
Someone who has never met your family can:
1. Tell from the top of page one what the collection is and what it covers.
2. Find a specific letter or photo and pull the right box.
3. See what's restricted, what's missing, and what's uncertain.
4. Trust it, because every claim is either sourced or marked as a guess.
