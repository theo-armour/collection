# Archiving a Family Collection

A family archive is a set of materials kept together, described, and looked after so that people decades from now can find them, trust them, and understand them. The work happens in roughly eight stages.

## 1. Survey and plan
- **Take stock before you touch anything.** Walk through every box, album, drawer, hard drive and phone. Record what each one holds, where it is, roughly how much there is, and what condition it's in.
- **Set the scope.** Decide whose lives and which years and branches the archive covers. Deciding what's out of scope matters as much as deciding what's in.
- **Triage by risk.** Deal first with whatever is at risk: mold, insect damage, crumbling newsprint, magnetic tape, failing drives, and elders whose memories haven't been recorded.

## 2. Appraise
- **Not everything is archival.** Keep what has evidential or emotional value: letters, diaries, certificates, photographs, recordings, deeds, military papers and recipes.
- **Weed carefully.** Remove duplicates, blank pages, and generic material such as tourist brochures or bank statements with no story attached. Keep a single example where it shows how life was lived.
- **Make a note of what you discard.** A list of removed items stops later doubt about what went missing.

## 3. Arrange
- **Provenance:** keep each person's or household's materials together. Don't blend Grandma's papers with Uncle Bill's.
- **Original order:** if someone filed things their own way, keep that order. It is evidence of how they thought.
- **Hierarchy:** organize by person or family, then series (correspondence, photographs, legal papers), then folder, then item.

## 4. Describe
- **Write a finding aid.** This is a guide to the collection, covering its scope, a biographical sketch, the arrangement, and a folder-level inventory.
- **Record the core details for each item:** who, what, where, when (`YYYY-MM-DD`, with "circa" where unsure), the source, and the condition.
- **Identify the people in photographs now.** Sit with older relatives and record names, places and stories. This knowledge disappears soonest and can't be recovered.
- **Use consistent names.** Full names with maiden names, and the same spelling everywhere.

## 5. Preserve the physical originals
- **Housing:** acid-free and lignin-free folders and boxes, and PAT-passed photo sleeves made of polyester, polypropylene or paper. Avoid PVC, "magnetic" albums, rubber bands, paper clips and tape.
- **Environment:** cool, dry and stable, about 65–70°F and 30–50% relative humidity. No attics, basements or outside walls, and keep it dark.
- **Handling:** clean, dry hands or nitrile gloves. Unfold flat and don't laminate.
- **Labeling:** soft pencil on folders. Never use ink on the backs of photos.
- **Get a conservator for anything badly damaged**, such as mold, water damage or brittle documents. Don't try home repairs.

## 6. Digitize
- **Resolution:** 600 ppi for photographs and 300–400 ppi for documents. Use higher resolution for slides, negatives and small prints.
- **Formats:** TIFF for the preservation master, plus JPEG or PDF copies for everyday viewing.
- **File names:** sortable names such as `1952-06-14-armour-wedding-001.tif`. Store the descriptive details inside the file as well (IPTC/XMP).
- **Audio and video:** cassettes, VHS and reels decay quickly. Send them out for transfer early.
- **Keep the originals afterwards.** A scan is a copy, not a replacement.

## 7. Preserve the digital files
- **3-2-1 rule:** three copies, on two kinds of media, with one copy off-site or in the cloud.
- **Open formats:** TIFF, PDF/A, WAV, MP4, plain text, CSV, and GEDCOM for genealogy.
- **Fixity:** create checksums so you can detect silent file corruption.
- **Refresh:** check the files every year and move them to new storage every 3–5 years.

## 8. Provide access and plan succession
- **Sharing:** a website, a shared drive, a printed book, or a donation to a historical society, university or local archive.
- **Privacy:** be careful with living people, medical and adoption records, and family conflicts. Restrict or delay access where needed.
- **Name a successor.** Write down who inherits the archive, where everything is, and how it's organized. An archive that only one person understands is at risk.
- **Rights:** record who holds copyright in photographs and letters, and what you have permission to publish.

## Guiding principles
Do no harm to originals. Keep the context. Record everything, including your own decisions. Prioritize what's most fragile and the memories that are most perishable. Finished and simple beats perfect and stalled.
