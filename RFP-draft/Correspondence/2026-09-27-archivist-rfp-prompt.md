## Role

You are an experienced archival consultant and a member of the Society of American Archivists (SAA). You know how Bay Area archivists read a request for proposal and what makes them want to respond.

## Task

Help me write a Request for Proposal (RFP) that I will post, under my own name, to:

* Society of California Archivists (SCA): https://calarchivists.org/
* West_Arch, the Western Archivists Listserv: https://groups.google.com/a/lists.berkeley.edu/g/west_arch

Produce three pieces:

1. **Introductory email** (150–250 words). Why I'm writing, what makes the collection interesting, what I'm looking for. Invite readers to open the attachment.
2. **The RFP** (2 pages, no more than 3). It will become a PDF attachment. Sections: overview, the collection, scope by phase, roles, deliverables, budget and terms, selection criteria, proposal requirements, contact and timeline.
3. **Optional supplement** (1–2 pages or a web link). A few representative photographs, links to my online work, and a short account of the family background.

## Style

* Plain, warm, first-person English.
* Use standard archival terms where they help: extent in linear feet, processing levels, MPLP, FADGI, OAIS / digital preservation. Keep it readable to a non-specialist.
* It should read like an invitation to join an intriguing archival project, not a bureaucratic procurement document.
* Don't invent facts. If something is missing, leave a clearly marked `[placeholder]`.

## What this RFP pays for

* **Contracted now: Phase 1, collection assessment and archival master plan.** Deliverables:
  * Inventory and condition assessment.
  * Recommended organization and cataloging system.
  * Digitization priorities, specifications, and estimated costs.
  * Digital preservation and access strategy, with privacy and ownership provisions.
  * Phased implementation schedule and budget.
* **Also requested in the proposal: how the respondent would lead Phases 2–3** (below). I'm choosing a long-term lead archivist, not just a report writer.

## Phases

* **Phase 1, Assessment**: an on-site survey with me present, then an extent and condition report and the master plan above.
* **Phase 2, Triage and digitization**: the archivist opens boxes and decides what each item is and where it goes: scan, specialist digitization, donation to an institution, sale, or recycling. Items are batched out to the right specialists: sheet-feed scanning, large-format photography, film and slide scanning, AV transfer, 3D or object photography.
* **Phase 3, Management and hosting**: a landing zone for files, consistent naming, basic item- or folder-level metadata, and transfer to long-term homes (Internet Archive, Permanent.org, GitHub) with integrity checks and redundancy. No ongoing hosting costs.
* **Placement**: help approaching Princeton (Norman Armour, Walter Lowrie), the Art Institute of Chicago (George Armour), and institutions interested in the Koudacheff material.

## Roles

* **The lead archivist manages; they don't have to do it all.** They source and coordinate specialists from their own network. I want to be an excellent client, not the project manager.
* **Disposition decisions**: we agree rules up front (for example, duplicate photos and generic manuals can be recycled). The archivist applies them and brings exceptions to me. Nothing is recycled until I've had a chance to review it.
* **Access decisions**: open by default. The consultant recommends a simple access policy (public / restricted / embargoed until a date) and flags sensitive material during the survey: third-party letters, living people, confidential business records, health, financial and legal papers. I make the final calls.
* **Description and curation after digitization**: done largely by me, with AI help, over the rest of my life.

## The collection

* **Extent**: about 200 linear feet, mostly bank boxes, in two indoor storage units in San Francisco. Oversize architectural drawings are extra.
* **Dates**: 1700s to now, mostly 1970s onward.
* **Condition and order**: order is quite mixed. There's some room in the units to open boxes. No known hazards (mold, pests, water damage), though the survey should confirm this.
* **Material types**: letters, postcards, photos, slides, negatives, architectural drawings (some large-format), business records, publications, clippings, notebooks, manuals, software, data media, audio.
* **Born-digital**: hard drives, Dropbox, Google Drive, GitHub. Legacy media is likely present but the extent is unknown: floppies, Zip disks, CDs/DVDs, audio and video tapes, old software such as AutoCAD. Ask how respondents would handle these, themselves or through partners (e.g. write-blocked disk imaging, BitCurator-style workflows, AV transfer vendors).
* **Art and objects**: about 30 works of art in a separate art storage facility, several of museum quality, plus furniture and objets d'art. An outdated appraisal exists. **In scope for documentation and provenance only**: photography, basic records, and finding provenance papers in the boxes. Monetary appraisal and placement are referred to qualified appraisers and art advisors.
* **Tax appraisals**: donations to institutions may need monetary appraisals. These are done by separate appraisers, referred out.
