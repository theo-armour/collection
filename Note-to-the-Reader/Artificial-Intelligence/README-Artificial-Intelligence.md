### Five ways AI may change the making of this archive

Theo> I'm in the process of employing a personal archivist. What are five ways that AI will disrupt the creation of a personal archive?

Fable 5.2> These are my working thoughts, not settled policy. I'd welcome an archivist's view on each.

1. **Describing gets cheap; verifying becomes the work.** AI can now transcribe handwriting, OCR letters, caption photos, suggest faces, and guess dates in bulk. The slow part used to be describing each item. Now it is catching the confident mistakes: the wrong cousin named, a misread year, a garbled place name.
2. **Search matters more than arrangement.** If I can ask "what did I write about the Bay Area in the 1980s?" and get an answer, deep folder trees matter less. What matters more is that everything is machine-readable: plain text, Markdown, open formats, sensible file names. A box of scans with no text layer is nearly invisible to AI.
3. **Originals and AI output must be kept apart.** Restored photos, colorized prints, AI summaries, and drafted captions will sit next to the originals. Unless they are marked, no one will be able to tell them apart in a generation. My hope is a simple rule: the original is never altered, anything machine-made is labeled, and the tool and date are recorded.
4. **Keeping everything becomes possible, so choosing becomes harder.** Space and time used to limit what an archive kept. AI removes most of those limits. It also makes everything findable, including things about living people that were private only because no one could dig them up. Deciding what to keep, restrict, or let go becomes the central task.
5. **The archive may be asked to speak.** A well-organized personal archive is exactly what someone needs to build a chatbot that imitates its subject. Some of this collection's future readers will be machines. I would rather decide now, in writing, who may query it and whether it may be used to imitate my voice, than leave that to others later.
