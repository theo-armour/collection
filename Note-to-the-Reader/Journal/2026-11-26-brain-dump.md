**Where and how to keep things?** In terms of large, huge repositories of lots of different kinds of information, it seems to me that Wikipedia is the reference object. Its database is just a very long list of unique names. Other reference materials are linked from the main article to Wikipedia Commons and other places like that.

I think my preference would be to have everything that belongs to a unique source kept in a folder. The folder is the unique identifier, a person's name and dates, or something like that. A README file has pretty much the same information as a Markdown file.

Any pictures, papers, or reference works can also be kept in that folder.

I kind of like what's happening with the Gedcom Viewer and the Genealogy repositories. GEDCOM viewer is more or less the card catalog of links. The Genealogy contains the Wikipedia-like biography and some source material as well

**Three areas of concern**

**Index** Where to find the sources. Card catalogs, gedcom files, word clouds, tags, etc. GEDCOM, Dewey Decimal, CSV files

**Article** An outline of that person, which then links to primary documents and secondary and third sources. Articles like Wikipedia. Folders

**Sources** Places with collections of data. Book shelves. Repositories, hard drives, cloud storage

**Follow GitHub**

You can access petabytes of source data using GitHub search. They seem to know what they're doing.

* https://github.com/search/
* https://github.com/search/advanced
* https://docs.github.com/en/search-github/getting-started-with-searching-on-github/about-searching-on-github

**Follow wikipedia**

You can access petabytes of data using Wikipedia search. They seem to know what they're doing.

* https://www.wikipedia.org/
* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special:Search
* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Contents

**Follow what?** It's not just about following GitHub and Wikipedia search capabilities. It's about how they store data, share data, promote data, and a bunch of other stuff on such a massive total scale. The other aspect is that they are modern. They are dealing with AI in modern, fresh, future-oriented ways. It's another way of looking at things that's different from Dublin Core, Library of Congress catalogs, and 3x5 cards. Putting my cards on the table, I'm much more invested in future-oriented ways. Even so, I will be delighted to learn about long-standing alternatives. Hm, and can we embed our knowledge in DNA?
